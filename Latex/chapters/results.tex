\section{Vanilla DQN Performance Ceiling}

Extended evaluation over 37,213 episodes of Phase~4 League Training followed the completion of the multi-phase curriculum. The Vanilla DQN agent, equipped with an LSTM-based history encoder, trained against a diverse opponent pool comprising random, greedy heuristic, league, and self-play opponents. Table~\ref{tab:vanilla_performance} summarizes the aggregate performance metrics.

Six empirical findings emerge from the training data. Each points to a distinct architectural limitation.

\paragraph{Finding 1: Win Rate Plateau.}
The cumulative win rate for Agent~1 converged to approximately 22\% within the first 2,000 episodes and showed no statistically significant improvement over the remaining 35,000 episodes. The 100-episode rolling win rate oscillated between 15\% and 30\% without sustaining any upward trend. The policy had reached a fixed point.

\paragraph{Finding 2: Loss--Performance Decoupling.}
The policy loss (temporal-difference error) decreased monotonically to approximately 0.001, demonstrating successful minimization of the Bellman residual. No corresponding increase in win rate accompanied this convergence. The agent achieved low prediction error by accurately estimating the value of passive, draw-oriented policies rather than discovering superior offensive strategies.

\paragraph{Finding 3: Q-Value Overestimation.}
The average Q-value rose from 0.2 to 1.2 over training despite no corresponding improvement in game outcomes. This monotonic increase in estimated action values without realized performance gains is characteristic of systematic overestimation bias.

\paragraph{Finding 4: LSTM Prediction Accuracy Ceiling.}
The LSTM-based piece identity inference module plateaued at approximately 18--22\% prediction accuracy. While this represents a 2.2--2.6$\times$ improvement over the random chance baseline of 8.3\% (given 12 piece types), it remains far below the accuracy required for reliable tactical decision-making. The prediction accuracy curve flattened after episode 5,000, with no further improvement observed despite continued training.

\paragraph{Finding 5: Opponent-Specific Performance Asymmetry.}
A pronounced disparity appeared across opponent types. The agent achieved only 15.2\% win rate against the greedy heuristic but 24.8\% in self-play. The learned policy closely adapted to its own behavioral distribution and failed to generalize to opponents employing different strategies.

\paragraph{Finding 6: Draw Dominance.}
The aggregate draw rate of 38.5\% substantially exceeded the win rate of 22.2\%. The agent adopted a risk-averse policy characterized by passive piece shuffling and avoidance of uncertain engagements. This behavior is consistent with an agent that learned to minimize short-term losses rather than maximize long-term winning probability.

\section{Behavioral Patterns from Training Logs}

Beyond the aggregate statistics, analysis of the 37,213-episode training logs reveals four recurring behavioral patterns that characterize the Vanilla DQN architecture's limitations.

\subsection{Passive Shuffling Convergence}

As training progressed, the agent developed an increasingly conservative movement policy. The average episode length grew steadily throughout training, reflecting a tendency to avoid initiating combat and instead cycle pieces between adjacent squares. This pattern is consistent with the scalar value collapse described in Finding~2: when the Q-network cannot distinguish high-variance aggressive moves from low-variance passive ones, the policy converges to the action class with the lowest expected variance, which in Stratego corresponds to non-combat positional shuffling.

\subsection{Opponent-Specific Adaptation Failure}

The performance disparity across opponent types (Table~\ref{tab:vanilla_performance}) reveals that the agent overfits to its own behavioral distribution. Against the greedy heuristic, which systematically attacks the nearest enemy piece regardless of rank uncertainty, the agent's passive shuffling is exploited by the opponent's willingness to initiate combat. In self-play, both agents adopt similar risk-averse policies, producing symmetric outcomes that inflate the apparent win rate. The fixed $\epsilon$-greedy exploration schedule provides no mechanism for state-dependent adaptation to novel opponent behaviors, preventing generalization across strategies.

\subsection{Loss-Reward Divergence}

The temporal-difference loss decreased from approximately 0.05 during early training to approximately 0.001 by episode 37,000, while the average episode reward remained flat at approximately 0.08 throughout. This divergence confirms that the network successfully learned to predict the outcomes of the passive policies it had already discovered, rather than exploring the policy space for strategies with higher expected returns. In reinforcement learning terms, the agent converged to an accurate value function for a suboptimal policy rather than discovering the optimal policy itself.

\subsection{LSTM Accuracy as an Information Barrier}

The LSTM piece prediction accuracy plateaued at approximately 20\% after episode 5,000 and showed no further improvement over the remaining 32,000 episodes. This ceiling imposes an upper bound on the agent's tactical capability. With only one in five hidden piece identities correctly inferred, the agent cannot reliably assess combat outcomes and defaults to avoidance. The persistence of this plateau despite continued gradient updates indicates an architectural limitation rather than insufficient training. The 64-dimensional hidden state lacks the representational capacity to maintain accurate belief estimates over 200 to 500 move sequences.


\section{Draw Dominance and Learning Signal Quality}

The prevalence of draws in the training results (38.5\% of all games) warrants analysis both as a performance metric and as a factor affecting learning signal quality.

In the reward structure, terminal outcomes carry the following values: flag capture victories receive $+1.0$, depletion victories receive $+0.5$, losses receive $-1.0$, and draws receive $-0.3$. A loss produces a gradient signal approximately 3.3 times stronger than a draw. Losses unambiguously identify failed strategies through a large negative reward. Draws produce a near-zero reward that provides minimal information about whether the preceding actions were strategically sound. An agent experiencing a higher proportion of losses relative to draws receives more informative feedback from which to update its policy.

The Vanilla DQN's preference for draw-producing strategies is a symptom of the scalar value collapse (Finding~2). The scalar Q-value cannot represent the multi-modal distribution of possible outcomes for a given action. The agent cannot distinguish between a move with a 40\% chance of winning and a 60\% chance of losing (expected value approximately $-0.2$) and a move with a 100\% chance of drawing (expected value $-0.3$). The draw-producing move often receives a higher Q-value estimate despite being strategically inferior, because the scalar mean fails to capture the risk-reward tradeoff inherent in aggressive play.

The draw dominance is not merely a consequence of the agent's inability to win. It is an active failure mode in which the agent suppresses the very feedback signals that would enable strategic improvement. A distributional value representation such as C51 would preserve the multi-modal structure of action outcomes and enable the policy to evaluate the tradeoff between safe draws and risky decisive actions.


\section{Architectural Comparison: Unified versus Dual-Model Pipeline}

An earlier iteration employed two separate neural network models: a dedicated SetupAgent trained through distributional reinforcement learning for initial piece placement, and a separate attack-phase DQN for movement and combat decisions. This dual-model architecture was abandoned in favor of the current unified pipeline, where a single DQN handles all movement decisions and a heuristic with randomized flag positioning manages piece placement. Table~\ref{tab:architecture_comparison} summarizes the differences.

\begin{table}[htbp]
    \centering
    \caption{Dual-model versus unified architectural pipelines.}
    \label{tab:architecture_comparison}
    \begin{tabular}{|p{3cm}|p{5.5cm}|p{5.5cm}|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Aspect} & \textbf{Dual-Model (Setup + Attack)} & \textbf{Unified DQN} \\
        \hline
        Architecture & Separate C51-based SetupAgent for placement, separate DQN for attack & Single DQN for movement; heuristic setup with randomized flag \\
        \hline
        Setup Latency & Approximately 2 seconds per episode & Under 100 milliseconds per episode \\
        \hline
        Training Overhead & Doubled compute requirement (setup and attack training phases) & Halved total training compute \\
        \hline
        Exploitability & Deterministic setup patterns learned by opponents & Randomized placement resists opponent exploitation \\
        \hline
        Draw Pattern & Draws due to gradient interference between competing objectives & Draws due to risk-averse policy under uncertainty \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The transition from dual-model to unified architecture yielded two principal insights. The SetupAgent tended to converge on deterministic placement configurations, introducing a predictability vulnerability. Opponent agents that trained against these fixed patterns could exploit the predictable flag positions and bomb arrangements. Replacing the learned setup agent with a heuristic that randomizes flag positioning eliminated this exploitability and reduced per-episode setup latency by a factor of twenty.

The dual-model architecture also suffered from gradient interference between competing optimization objectives. The setup model received gradients rewarding defensive configurations, while the attack model received gradients rewarding aggressive piece utilization. These objectives were partially contradictory: optimal defense often places high-value pieces in positions suboptimal for attack. The unified architecture removed this conflict by excluding the setup phase from the learning loop, directing all gradient signal toward improving movement and combat decisions.

Both architectures converged to draw-dominated outcomes, though for different reasons. In the dual-model case, gradient interference between setup and attack objectives prevented either model from developing a coherent strategy. In the unified case, scalar value collapse and exploration deficiencies produced the risk-averse shuffling behavior documented in the preceding sections. The draw dominance is attributable to fundamental architectural properties of the DQN rather than to engineering decisions at the pipeline level.


\section{Architectural Bottleneck Analysis}

The empirical findings above are not incidental outcomes of insufficient training or suboptimal hyperparameters. Each observed limitation is an expected consequence of the Vanilla DQN architecture's structural properties. This section draws on established theoretical results from the reinforcement learning literature.

\subsection{Q-Value Overestimation}

The systematic Q-value inflation in Finding~3 is a well-characterized property of single-estimator Q-learning. Standard DQN uses the same network $Q_\theta$ for both action selection and value evaluation:

\begin{equation}
y_t = r_t + \gamma \max_{a'} Q_\theta(s_{t+1}, a')
\end{equation}

When Q-value estimates contain approximation noise $\epsilon_a \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$, the max operator introduces a systematic positive bias \cite{RainbowJMLR2021}:

\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{a} (Q^*(s, a) + \epsilon_a)\right] \geq \max_{a} Q^*(s, a)
\end{equation}

This follows from the convexity of the max function and Jensen's inequality. The bias increases with both the variance of the noise and the cardinality of the action space $|\mathcal{A}|$. In Stratego, where the legal action space can exceed 200 moves per turn, this overestimation is substantial. Double DQN decouples action selection from value evaluation, provably reducing the bias through two independent estimators \cite{RainbowJMLR2021}.

\subsection{Scalar Value Collapse}

Finding~2, where loss convergence failed to produce corresponding performance improvement, is explained through distributional reinforcement learning theory. Standard DQN approximates the expected return $\mathbb{E}[Z(s,a)]$ using scalar Q-values and minimizes the mean squared Bellman error:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}\left[\left(r + \gamma \max_{a'} Q(s', a') - Q(s, a)\right)^2\right]
\end{equation}

Distributional reinforcement learning theory shows that in stochastic environments, the full return distribution $Z(s,a)$ may be multi-modal \cite{RainbowJMLR2021}. In Stratego, outcomes cluster into distinct modes: decisive victories ($R \approx +1$), decisive losses ($R \approx -1$), material exchanges ($R \in [-0.5, +0.5]$), and draws ($R \approx 0$). A scalar Q-value collapses these modes into a single mean, which may not correspond to any realizable outcome. The loss function converges when this mean is correctly estimated, even if the policy cannot distinguish between high-variance aggressive strategies and low-variance passive ones.

Categorical DQN (C51) models the value distribution over 51 discrete atoms. The projected distributional Bellman operator is a contraction in the Cram\'{e}r distance, establishing formal convergence guarantees \cite{RainbowJMLR2021}.

\subsection{LSTM Information Bottleneck}

The LSTM prediction accuracy ceiling in Finding~4 stems from the sequential compression inherent in recurrent architectures. The LSTM processes the observation sequence $o_1, o_2, \ldots, o_T$ through a fixed-width hidden state $h_t \in \mathbb{R}^{64}$, which must encode all strategically relevant information from the entire history at each timestep:

\begin{equation}
h_t = \text{LSTM}(o_t, h_{t-1})
\end{equation}

For a typical Stratego game lasting $T = 200$ to $500$ moves, the hidden state must simultaneously maintain belief estimates over 40 opponent pieces across 12 possible types. The theoretical information capacity of a 64-dimensional continuous vector suffices in principle, but the sequential update imposes a practical constraint. Information from early observations $o_k$ (for small $k$) must survive $T - k$ successive nonlinear transformations to influence the final representation. Despite gating mechanisms designed to mitigate vanishing gradients, information retention degrades with sequence length \cite{Aaren}.

Attention-based architectures bypass this bottleneck by computing direct weighted aggregations over the full history. The AAREN module maintains $O(1)$ inference complexity per timestep while providing attention-weighted access to all prior observations:

\begin{equation}
h_t = \frac{a_t}{c_t} = \frac{\sum_{k=1}^{t} v_k \exp(s_k - m_t)}{\sum_{k=1}^{t} \exp(s_k - m_t)}
\end{equation}

This formulation eliminates the sequential compression requirement. The network attends directly to observations from any prior timestep without information degradation.

\subsection{Exploration Collapse}

The flat Noisy Net sigma in the training metrics (Figure~\ref{fig:training_diagnostics_37k}) reveals that the agent's exploration capacity collapsed. The Vanilla DQN uses $\epsilon$-greedy exploration with a fixed decay from $\epsilon = 1.0$ to $\epsilon_{\min} = 0.01$. Once the schedule is exhausted, the agent explores with a spatially uniform probability of 1\% regardless of state.

Stratego's state space exceeds $10^{535}$ configurations \cite{perolat2022stratego}. Uniform random exploration is exponentially unlikely to discover improved strategies in unexplored regions. Noisy Networks \cite{RainbowJMLR2021} parameterize exploration noise as learnable per-weight perturbations $\sigma_w$, enabling state-dependent exploration that concentrates uncertainty on states where the value function is unreliable.

\subsection{Sparse Reward Propagation}

The inability of the Vanilla DQN to develop long-horizon strategic behavior (as evidenced by Findings~1, 5, and 6) is compounded by its reliance on single-step temporal-difference learning. In standard Q-learning, the value information from a terminal reward at step $T$ propagates backward through the Bellman backup at a rate governed by the discount factor $\gamma$:

\begin{equation}
Q(s_k, a_k) \leftarrow Q(s_k, a_k) + \alpha \left( r_k + \gamma Q(s_{k+1}, a_{k+1}) - Q(s_k, a_k) \right)
\end{equation}

For a decisive game outcome occurring at step $T$ in a game of length 300, a mid-game decision at step 100 receives an effective signal of $\gamma^{200}$. With $\gamma = 0.99$, this yields $0.99^{200} \approx 0.134$, meaning that only 13.4\% of the terminal reward signal reaches mid-game decisions through single-step bootstrapping. Multi-step returns ($n$-step TD) \cite{RainbowJMLR2021} propagate terminal signals $n$ times faster by directly incorporating rewards over $n$ consecutive steps:

\begin{equation}
y_t^{(n)} = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \gamma^k r_{t+k} + \gamma^n Q(s_{t+n}, a_{t+n})
\end{equation}

This reduces the effective bootstrap depth and accelerates the propagation of sparse terminal rewards to earlier decision points.

\section{Supporting Evidence from Related Work}

The limitations identified above are consistent with prior findings in deep reinforcement learning applied to imperfect information games of comparable complexity.

P\'{e}rolat et al. (2022) achieved expert-level play in full-size Stratego with DeepNash \cite{perolat2022stratego}, using a fundamentally different approach from scalar value-based methods. DeepNash employed game-theoretic regularization with a deep convolutional backbone, relying on regularized Nash dynamics (R-NaD) rather than Q-value maximization. DeepNash demonstrates that Stratego is solvable by deep RL, but architectural design must explicitly address non-stationarity and strategic diversity. The Ataraxos system extended this paradigm to no-press Diplomacy, achieving superhuman performance through KL-divergence stabilization with time-varying coefficients \cite{Ataraxos2025}. This regularization mechanism is analogous to the dynamic damping proposed in MARQ but absent from the Vanilla DQN baseline.

Hessel et al. (2021) demonstrated in the Rainbow DQN study that each individual enhancement (Double DQN, Prioritized Experience Replay, Dueling Networks, Noisy Nets, C51, and multi-step returns) provides additive performance improvements, with the combined agent outperforming any single-component variant \cite{RainbowJMLR2021}. The ablation study in that work is particularly relevant: removing any single component degraded performance, confirming that the architectural limitations identified here cannot be resolved by addressing any single bottleneck in isolation.
