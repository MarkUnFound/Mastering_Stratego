\section{Architectural Rationale: LSTM History Encoding vs.\ Full DRQN}

A key architectural decision in designing the Stratego agent concerns how sequential information---specifically the history of opponent actions---should be integrated into the Deep Q-Network. Two principal approaches exist: (1)~a \textbf{full Deep Recurrent Q-Network (DRQN)}, where the Q-network itself contains recurrent layers and carries hidden state across timesteps, and (2)~an \textbf{LSTM-based per-position history encoder}, where the LSTM operates as a separate module that encodes action sequences into fixed-size embeddings consumed by a feed-forward DQN.

We adopt the latter approach for several principled reasons:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Replay Buffer Compatibility.} Standard experience replay, a cornerstone of DQN stability, stores individual transitions $(s, a, r, s')$. A full DRQN requires either (a)~storing and replaying contiguous episode segments with their associated hidden states, breaking the i.i.d.\ sampling assumption, or (b)~``burning in'' hidden states by processing a prefix of each sampled sequence, which incurs substantial computational overhead. Our approach stores compact LSTM embedding snapshots alongside 15-channel board tensors, preserving the standard replay buffer infrastructure without modification.

    \item \textbf{Per-Position Granularity.} In Stratego, the critical sequential signal is \emph{per-piece}: the history of movements for each individual opponent piece reveals its likely identity (e.g., a piece that moves multiple squares is a Scout). A monolithic DRQN hidden state would conflate action histories across all 40 opponent pieces into a single vector, losing this spatial specificity. Our approach maintains independent LSTM hidden states for each of the 100 board positions, producing a spatially structured $(64, 10, 10)$ embedding tensor that preserves piece-level temporal information.

    \item \textbf{Gradient Isolation.} Separating the history encoder from the Q-network allows independent optimization: the LSTM receives gradients from both supervised cross-entropy loss (on revealed piece identities) and end-to-end DQN loss (via a gradient bridge mechanism). A full DRQN would couple these objectives within a single recurrent computation, potentially causing gradient interference between value estimation and history encoding.

    \item \textbf{Computational Efficiency.} The LSTM encoder processes only positions with observed enemy actions (typically 5--15 active positions per turn), whereas a DRQN would process the full board state recurrently at every timestep. This sparsity reduces per-step LSTM computation by an order of magnitude compared to a full DRQN forward pass.
\end{enumerate}

This design aligns with DeepNash's approach to Stratego \cite{Perolat2022}, where history information is encoded by a separate module and concatenated with board features, rather than being processed recurrently within the value network itself.

\section{Recommendations}

To further advance the capabilities of agents in imperfect information environments like Stratego, the integration of sequence modeling architectures—explicitly Transformers and Large Language Models (LLMs)—presents a substantial area of opportunity. These architectures offer sophisticated reasoning capabilities and structural scaffolding that could significantly improve decision-making in high-dimensional, sparse-reward spaces. Utilizing LLM-driven heuristics or Transformer-based sequence modeling is anticipated to yield a more robust, human-like strategic evaluation and improve the interpretability of hidden game states compared to standard deep reinforcement learning methodologies.
